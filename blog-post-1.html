Life and Uncertainty

Personal Grad School

Exactly 2 years ago, I moved to British Columbia. I remember that day clear as day because it marked the beginning of a new chapter in life, something I was working hard towards, and at the same time, could not possibly have predicted. I had come from Delhi to Canada to begin my Master's in Statistics at SFU. I had packed my life into three suitcases, and beneath the excitement had zero clue on what was next specially didn't see the massive wave of imposter syndrome flooding my brain. I did not know what my life in Canada would look like. I did not know what kind of researcher I would become, or if I could ever truly become one, the nerves were real. Two years later, I'm still finding my footing, still asking those questions, still curious, but I think I wouldn't replace my curiosity, those nerves for anything. I think that learning about uncertainty, the good fortune of not knowing combined with the drive to seek answers is one of the things that connects my experience of moving to British Columbia, to my experience as an academic, and even the way I think about the universe. It is also something I truly to appreciate.

When I first arrived in B.C., almost everything felt unfamiliar yet exciting. British Columbia was different. There were mountains in the distance. There were forests everywhere. The air felt different. I remember the sky from my first night and how calm it made me feel. Next few weeks pretty much flew by in trying to learn my way aounnd the city. Basic tasks that had once been automatic became different. I think that is one of the strangest parts of moving somewhere new. You do not realize how much of your life is changing, the evolution, growth isn't instantaneous but substantial. For a while, I was constantly figuring things out. And then, slowly, I wasn't. SFU university became home. Errands became routine. I developed favourite places and routines. The beautiful landscape that had initially seemed foreign became part of the background of my everyday life.

It turns out that becoming an academic is not particularly different. When I began graduate school, I thought I was becoming better at finding answers. Instead, I am learning that research is largely about becoming comfortable with questions that do not have obvious answers. Infact, I would say asking questions is probablly the most exciting part of research for me. My research project has taken me into Bayesian inference, computational statistics, Gaussian Processes, scalable methods, and astrophysics. There is something wonderfully appropriate about working in a field where uncertainty is not an inconvenience to be removed but simply a metric we're trying to quantify and minimize. It is the object of study. In statistics, we often want to understand not only what we think is happening, but how uncertain we should be about that belief. That idea of uncertainty quantification has become increasingly meaningful to me outside of statistics too.

There is another reason I find astrophysics particularly comforting. My research deals with stellar evolution, which means that I spend a considerable amount of time thinking about objects whose lives unfold over timescales that are almost impossible to comprehend. Stars are born, evolve ro red giants over millions or billions of years. The light we observe from distant objects has travelled enormous distances before reaching us. When I think about these systems, my own sense of time and scale changes. And sometimes, when I am stressed about something very small in my own life, that change in scale helps. Processes unfolding on scales so much larger than my own life that it becomes almost absurd to compare them. It reminds me that I am a very small part of something extraordinarily large. That's exactly what the ocean does to me aas well. The magnanomity of these is calming.

Perhaps the biggest lesson of my time in Canada has been learning that an academic life is not a straight line. It's a marathon not a sprint. There are moments when research feels exciting and everything seems to fit together. There are other moments when a problem refuses to cooperate, an idea does not work, or I realize that I understand much less than I thought I did. I have learned that this is not necessarily evidence that I am doing something wrong and that pressure is a priviledge. It is part of the process and not knowing is a key step in learning.
