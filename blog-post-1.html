Life and Uncertainty

Personal Grad School

Exactly 2 years ago, I moved to British Columbia. I remember that day clear as day because it marked the beginning of a new chapter in life, something I was working hard towards, and at the same time, could not possibly have predicted. I had come from Delhi to Canada to begin my Master's in Statistics at SFU. I had packed my life into three suitcases, and beneath the excitement had zero clue on what was next specially didn't see the massive wave of imposter syndrome flooding my brain. I did not know what my life in Canada would look like. I did not know what kind of researcher I would become, or if I could ever truly become one, the nerves were real. Two years later, I'm still finding my footing, still asking those questions, still curious, but I think I wouldn't replace my curiosity, those nerves for anything. I think that learning about uncertainty, the good fortune of not knowing combined with the drive to seek answers is one of the things that connects my experience of moving to British Columbia, to my experience as an academic, and even the way I think about the universe. It is also something I truly to appreciate.

When I first arrived in B.C., almost everything felt unfamiliar yet exciting. British Columbia was different. There were mountains in the distance. There were forests everywhere. The air felt different. I remember the sky from my first night and how calm it made me feel. Next few weeks pretty much flew by in trying to learn my way aounnd the city. Basic tasks that had once been automatic became different. I think that is one of the strangest parts of moving somewhere new. You do not realize how much of your life is changing, the evolution, growth isn't instantaneous but substantial. For a while, I was constantly figuring things out. And then, slowly, I wasn't. SFU university became home. Errands became routine. I developed favourite places and routines. The beautiful landscape that had initially seemed foreign became part of the background of my everyday life.

It turns out that becoming an academic is not particularly different.

When I began graduate school, I thought I was becoming better at finding answers.

Instead, I learned that research is largely about becoming comfortable with questions that do not have obvious answers.

My MSc in Statistics has taken me into Bayesian inference, computational statistics, Gaussian Processes, scalable methods, and astrophysics. My research focuses on stellar evolution models, where I use a fast emulator and Deep Gaussian Processes for emulation and Bayesian calibration.

There is something wonderfully appropriate about working in a field where uncertainty is not an inconvenience to be removed.

It is the object of study.

In statistics, we often want to understand not only what we think is happening, but how uncertain we should be about that belief.

That idea — uncertainty quantification — has become increasingly meaningful to me outside of statistics too.

I used to think uncertainty was something I wanted to get rid of.

Now I think of it differently.

Sometimes the goal is not to know exactly what will happen.

Sometimes the goal is to understand what you know, what you do not know, and how much confidence you should place in either.

That is useful in research.

It is also useful in life.

Looking at the Universe

There is another reason I find astrophysics particularly comforting.

My research deals with stellar evolution, which means that I spend a considerable amount of time thinking about objects whose lives unfold over timescales that are almost impossible to comprehend.

Stars are born, evolve, and die over millions or billions of years.

The light we observe from distant objects has travelled enormous distances before reaching us. When I think about these systems, my own sense of time and scale changes.

And sometimes, when I am stressed about something very small in my own life, that change in scale helps.

A difficult day of research can feel enormous when I am sitting at my desk.

An uncertain email can feel important.

A mistake in my code can feel like a crisis.

A deadline can occupy far too much of my mind.

And then I remember what I am actually studying.

Stars.

Galaxies.

The history of the universe.

Processes unfolding on scales so much larger than my own life that it becomes almost absurd to compare them.

That does not make my problems disappear.

A bad day is still a bad day. Uncertainty about my future is still uncertainty. Academic work can still be frustrating, and being far from home can still be difficult.

But thinking about the universe gives those things a different scale.

It reminds me that I am a very small part of something extraordinarily large.

For me, that is not depressing.

It is calming.

There is something liberating about realizing that not everything needs to be resolved immediately.

Learning to Be an Academic

Perhaps the biggest lesson of my time in Canada has been learning that an academic life is not a straight line.

There are moments when research feels exciting and everything seems to fit together. There are other moments when a problem refuses to cooperate, an idea does not work, or I realize that I understand much less than I thought I did.

I have learned that this is not necessarily evidence that I am doing something wrong.

It is part of the process.

The same has been true of teaching.

As a teaching assistant, I have sometimes watched a student struggle with a concept and then suddenly understand it. Those moments have reminded me that learning is rarely instantaneous. Understanding often emerges gradually, through uncertainty, confusion, questions, and eventually clarity.

That process feels familiar to me now.

I have experienced it myself countless times.

I think becoming an academic has therefore taught me something beyond statistics: not knowing is not the opposite of learning. It is where learning begins.

A Different Relationship with Uncertainty

When I think back to the person who arrived in British Columbia in August 2024, I realize that she was trying very hard to predict what would happen next.

I wanted to know whether I would succeed.

I wanted to know what my research would become.

I wanted to know where I would live, what opportunities would come next, and what kind of person I would be on the other side of the experience.

I still do not know all of those things.

But I am much more comfortable with that now.

Perhaps that is the strange connection between moving across the world, becoming a statistician, and studying the universe.

All three have taught me that uncertainty is unavoidable.

You can try to quantify it. You can model it. You can prepare for it. You can study it.

But you cannot completely eliminate it.

And maybe you are not supposed to.

When I look back at my move to British Columbia, I no longer see it simply as the moment I left one country and arrived in another.

I see it as the beginning of a period in which I learned to live without knowing exactly what comes next.

I came to Canada to study statistics.

Somewhere along the way, statistics began teaching me how to think about my own life.

I still worry about the small things. I still have difficult days. I still have moments when the uncertainty feels uncomfortable.

But sometimes I step outside, look toward the mountains, think about the stars I spend my days studying, and remember the scale of the universe.

For a moment, my problems become smaller.

Not because they do not matter.

But because I remember that I do not need to know everything that comes next.

I only need to keep moving through the uncertainty.

And perhaps that is what growing up, doing research, and building a life in a new country have all taught me:

We do not need certainty to move forward. We need enough understanding to take the next step.
